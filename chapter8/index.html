Variants of Differential Privacy¶

Learning Objectives

After reading this chapter, you will be able to:

  • Define Rényi differential privacy and zero-concentrated differential privacy

  • Describe the advantages of these variants over (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)(ϵ,δ)-differential privacy

  • Convert privacy costs from these variants into (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)(ϵ,δ)-differential privacy

Recall that most of the bounds on privacy cost we have shown are upper bounds, but they sometimes represent very loose upper bounds - the true privacy cost is much less than the upper bound says. The primary motivation in developing new variants of differential privacy is to enable tighter bounds on privacy cost - especially for iterative algorithms - while maintaining privacy definitions which are useful in practice. For example, the catastrophic failure mode of (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)(ϵ,δ)-differential privacy is not desirable; the variants we’ll see in this section enable even tighter composition for some kinds of queries, while at the same time eliminating the catastrophic failure mode.

Let’s take a quick look at the tools we have already seen; we’ll look first at (non-adaptive) sequential composition for ϵ\epsilonϵ-differential privacy. It turns out that sequential composition for ϵ\epsilonϵ-differential privacy is tight. What does that mean? It means there’s a counterexample that would fail to satisfy any lower bound:

  • A mechanism FFF exists which satisfies ϵ\epsilonϵ-differential privacy

  • When composed kkk times, FFF satisfies kϵk\epsilonkϵ-differential privacy

  • But FFF does not satisfy cϵc\epsiloncϵ-differential privacy for any c<kc < kc<k

A neat way to visualize this is to look at what happens to privacy cost when we “vectorize” a query: that is, we merge lots of queries into a single query which returns a vector of the individual answers. Because the answer is a vector, we can use the vector-valued Laplace mechanism just once, and avoid composition altogether. Below, we’ll graph how much noise is needed for kkk queries, first under sequential composition, and then using the “vectorized” form. In the sequential composition case, each query has a sensitivity of 1, so the scale of the noise for each one is 1ϵi\frac{1}{\epsilon_i}ϵi​1​. If we want a total privacy cost of ϵ\epsilonϵ, then the ϵi\epsilon_iϵi​s must add up to ϵ\epsilonϵ, so ϵi=ϵk\epsilon_i = \frac{\epsilon}{k}ϵi​=kϵ​. This means that each query gets Laplace noise with scale kϵ\frac{k}{\epsilon}ϵk​. In the “vectorized” case, there’s just one query, but it has an L1L1L1 sensitivity of ∑i=1k1=k\sum_{i=1}^k 1 = k∑i=1k​1=k, so the scale of the noise is kϵ\frac{k}{\epsilon}ϵk​ in this case too.

The two lines overlap completely. This means that no matter how many queries we’re running, under ϵ\epsilonϵ-differential privacy, we can’t do any better than sequential composition. That’s because sequential composition is just as good as vectorizing the query, effectively converting it to a single query without involving composition, and we can’t do any better than that.

What about (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)(ϵ,δ)-differential privacy? The story is a little different there. In the sequential composition case, we can use advanced composition; we have to be a little careful to ensure that the total privacy cost is exactly (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)(ϵ,δ). Specifically, we set ϵi=ϵ22klog⁡(1/δ′)\epsilon_i = \frac{\epsilon}{2 \sqrt{2k \log(1/\delta')}}ϵi​=22klog(1/δ′)​ϵ​, δi=δ2k\delta_i = \frac{\delta}{2k}δi​=2kδ​, and δ′=δ2\delta' = \frac{\delta}{2}δ′=2δ​ (splitting δ\deltaδ to go 50% towards the queries, and 50% towards advanced composition). By advanced composition, the total privacy cost for all kkk queries is (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)(ϵ,δ). The scale of the noise, by the Gaussian mechanism, is:

σ2=2log⁡(1.25δi)ϵi2=16klog⁡(1δ′)log⁡(1.25δi)ϵ2=16klog⁡(2δ)log⁡(2.5kδ)ϵ2\begin{align} \sigma^2 =& \frac{2 \log\left(\frac{1.25}{\delta_i}\right)}{\epsilon_i^2}\\ =& \frac{16 k \log\left(\frac{1}{\delta'}\right) \log\left(\frac{1.25}{\delta_i}\right)}{\epsilon^2}\\ =& \frac{16 k \log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right) \log\left(\frac{2.5 k}{\delta}\right)}{\epsilon^2}\\ \end{align}σ2===​ϵi2​2log(δi​1.25​)​ϵ216klog(δ′1​)log(δi​1.25​)​ϵ216klog(δ2​)log(δ2.5k​)​​​

In the “vectorized” case, we have just one query, with an L2L2L2 sensitivity of k\sqrt{k}k​. The scale of the noise, by the Gaussian mechanism, is σ2=2klog⁡(1.25/δ)ϵ2\sigma^2 = \frac{2 k \log(1.25/\delta)}{\epsilon^2}σ2=ϵ22klog(1.25/δ)​.

What does this difference mean in practice? The two behave the same asymptotically in kkk, but have different constants, and the advanced composition case has an additional logarithmic factor in δ\deltaδ. All this adds up to a much looser bound in the case of advanced composition. Let’s graph the two as we did before.

Max Divergence and Rényi Divergence¶

It turns out that the definition of differential privacy can be stated directly in terms of something called max divergence. In statistics, a divergence is a way of measuring the distance between two probability distributions - which is exactly what we want to do for differential privacy. The max divergence is the worst-case analog of the Kullback–Leibler divergence, one of the most common such measures. The max divergence between two probability distributions YYY and ZZZ is defined to be:

Definition 1 (Max Divergence)
D∞(Y∥Z)=max⁡S⊆Supp(Y)[log⁡Pr[Y∈S]Pr[Z∈S]]\begin{align*} D_\infty(Y \Vert Z) = \max_{S \subseteq \text{Supp}(Y)} \Big[\log \frac{Pr[Y \in S]}{Pr[Z \in S]} \Big] \end{align*}D∞​(Y∥Z)=S⊆Supp(Y)max​[logPr[Z∈S]Pr[Y∈S]​]​
(2)

This already looks a lot like the condition for ϵ\epsilonϵ-differential privacy! In particular, it turns out that FFF satisfies ϵ\epsilonϵ-differential privacy if:

D∞(F(x)∥F(x′))≤ϵ\begin{align} D_\infty(F(x) \Vert F(x')) \leq \epsilon \end{align}D∞​(F(x)∥F(x′))≤ϵ​​
(3)

An interesting direction for research in differential privacy is the exploration of alternative privacy definitions in terms of other divergences. Of these, the Rényi divergence is particularly interesting, since it also (like max divergence) allows us to recover the original definition of differential privacy. The Rényi divergence of order α\alphaα between probability distributions PPP and QQQ is defined as (where P(x)P(x)P(x) and Q(x)Q(x)Q(x) denote the probability density of PPP and QQQ at point xxx, respectively):

Definition 2 (Renyi Divergence)
Dα(P∥Q)=1α−1log⁡Ex∼Q(P(x)Q(x))α\begin{align*} D_\alpha(P \Vert Q) = \frac{1}{\alpha - 1} \log E_{x \sim Q} \Big(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\Big)^\alpha \end{align*}Dα​(P∥Q)=α−11​logEx∼Q​(Q(x)P(x)​)α​
(4)

If we set α=∞\alpha = \inftyα=∞, then we immediately recover the definition of ϵ\epsilonϵ-differential privacy! The obvious question arises: what happens if we set α\alphaα to something else? As we’ll see, it’s possible to use the Rényi divergence to derive really interesting relaxations of differential privacy that allow better composition theorems while at the same time avoiding the possibility of “catastrophe” which is possible under (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)(ϵ,δ)-differential privacy.

Rényi Differential Privacy¶

In 2017, Ilya Mironov proposed Rényi differential privacy (RDP) Mironov (2017). A randomized mechanism FFF satisfies (α,ϵˉ)(\alpha, \bar{\epsilon})(α,ϵˉ)-RDP if for all neighboring datasets xxx and x′x'x′

Dα(F(x)∥F(x′))≤ϵˉ\begin{align} D_\alpha(F(x) \Vert F(x')) \leq \bar{\epsilon} \end{align}Dα​(F(x)∥F(x′))≤ϵˉ​​
(5)

In other words, RDP requires that the Rényi divergence of order α\alphaα between F(x)F(x)F(x) and F(x′)F(x')F(x′) to be bounded by ϵˉ\bar{\epsilon}ϵˉ. Note that we’ll use ϵˉ\bar{\epsilon}ϵˉ to denote the ϵ\epsilonϵ parameter of RDP, in order to distinguish it from the ϵ\epsilonϵ in pure ϵ\epsilonϵ-differential privacy and (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)(ϵ,δ)-differential privacy.

A key property of Rényi differential privacy is that a mechanism which satisfies RDP also satisfies (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)(ϵ,δ)-differential privacy. Specifically, if FFF satisfies (α,ϵˉ)(\alpha, \bar{\epsilon})(α,ϵˉ)-RDP, then for δ>0\delta > 0δ>0, FFF satisfies (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)(ϵ,δ)-differential privacy for ϵ=ϵˉ+log⁡(1/δ)α−1\epsilon = \bar{\epsilon} + \frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\alpha - 1}ϵ=ϵˉ+α−1log(1/δ)​. The analyst is free to pick any value of δ\deltaδ; a meaningful value (e.g. δ≤1n2\delta \leq \frac{1}{n^2}δ≤n21​) should be picked in practice.

The basic mechanism for achieving Rényi differential privacy is the Gaussian mechanism. Specifically, for a function f:D→Rkf : \mathcal{D} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^kf:D→Rk with L2L2L2 sensitivity Δf\Delta fΔf, the following mechanism satisfies (α,ϵˉ)(\alpha, \bar{\epsilon})(α,ϵˉ)-RDP:

Definition 3 (Renyi Gaussian)
F(x)=f(x)+N(σ2) where σ2=(Δf)2α2ϵˉ\begin{align*} F(x) = f(x) + \mathcal{N}(\sigma^2) \text{ where } \sigma^2 = \frac{(\Delta f)^2 \alpha}{2\bar{\epsilon}} \end{align*}F(x)=f(x)+N(σ2) where σ2=2ϵˉ(Δf)2α​​
(6)

We can implement the Gaussian mechanism for Rényi differential privacy as follows:

def gaussian_mech_RDP_vec(vec, sensitivity, alpha, epsilon_bar):
    sigma = np.sqrt((sensitivity**2 * alpha) / (2 * epsilon_bar))
    return [v + np.random.normal(loc=0, scale=sigma) for v in vec]

The major advantage of Rényi differential privacy is tight composition for the Gaussian mechanism - and this advantage in composition comes without the need for a special advanced composition theorem. The sequential composition theorem of Rényi differential privacy states that:

Theorem 1 (Renyi-Sequential-Composition)

  • If F1F_1F1​ satisfies (α,ϵ1ˉ)(\alpha, \bar{\epsilon_1})(α,ϵ1​ˉ​)-RDP

  • And F2F_2F2​ satisfies (α,ϵ2ˉ)(\alpha, \bar{\epsilon_2})(α,ϵ2​ˉ​)-RDP

  • Then their composition satisfies (α,ϵ1ˉ+ϵ2ˉ)(\alpha, \bar{\epsilon_1} + \bar{\epsilon_2})(α,ϵ1​ˉ​+ϵ2​ˉ​)-RDP

Based on this sequential composition theorem, running an (α,ϵˉ)(\alpha, \bar{\epsilon})(α,ϵˉ)-RDP mechanism kkk times results in (α,kϵˉ)(\alpha, k\bar{\epsilon})(α,kϵˉ)-RDP. For a given level of noise (i.e. a given value for σ2\sigma^2σ2), bounding the privacy cost of repeated applications of the Gaussian mechanism using RDP’s sequential composition, and then converting to (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)(ϵ,δ)-differential privacy, will usually yield a much lower privacy cost than performing the composition directly in (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)(ϵ,δ) world (even with advanced composition). We will see a visualization which demonstrates this in the section after next.

As a result, the ideas behind Rényi differential privacy have been used to greatly improve the privacy cost accounting in a number of recent iterative algorithms, including Google’s differentially private version of Tensorflow.

Finally, like other variants of differential privacy, RDP provides a post-processing property.

Zero-Concentrated Differential Privacy¶

In concurrent work released in 2016, Mark Bun and Thomas Steinke proposed zero-concentrated differential privacy (zCDP) Bun & Steinke (2016). Like RDP, zCDP is defined in terms of the Rényi divergence, but it includes only a single privacy parameter (ρ\rhoρ). A randomized mechanism FFF satisfies ρ\rhoρ-zCDP if for all neighboring datasets xxx and x′x'x′, and all α∈(1,∞)\alpha \in (1, \infty)α∈(1,∞):

Dα(F(x)∥F(x′))≤ρα\begin{align} D_\alpha (F(x) \Vert F(x')) \leq \rho\alpha \end{align}Dα​(F(x)∥F(x′))≤ρα​​
(7)

This is a stronger requirement than RDP, because it restricts the Rényi divergence of many orders; however, the bound becomes more relaxed as α\alphaα grows. Like RDP, zCDP can be converted to (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)(ϵ,δ)-differential privacy: if FFF satisfies ρ\rhoρ-zCDP, then for δ>0\delta > 0δ>0, FFF satisfies (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)(ϵ,δ)-differential privacy for ϵ=ρ+2ρlog⁡(1/δ)\epsilon = \rho + 2\sqrt{\rho \log(1/\delta)}ϵ=ρ+2ρlog(1/δ)​.

zCDP is also similar to RDP in that the Gaussian mechanism can be used as a basic mechanism. Specifically, for a function f:D→Rkf : \mathcal{D} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^kf:D→Rk with L2L2L2 sensitivity Δf\Delta fΔf, the following mechanism satisfies ρ\rhoρ-zCDP:

Definition 4 (zCDP Gaussian)
F(x)=f(x)+N(σ2) where σ2=(Δf)22ρ\begin{align*} F(x) = f(x) + \mathcal{N}(\sigma^2) \text{ where } \sigma^2 = \frac{(\Delta f)^2}{2\rho} \end{align*}F(x)=f(x)+N(σ2) where σ2=2ρ(Δf)2​​
(8)

As with RDP, this mechanism is easy to implement:

def gaussian_mech_zCDP_vec(vec, sensitivity, rho):
    sigma = np.sqrt((sensitivity**2) / (2 * rho))
    return [v + np.random.normal(loc=0, scale=sigma) for v in vec]

In another similarity with RDP, zCDP’s sequential composition is also asymptotically tight for repeated applications of the Gaussian mechanism. It’s also very simple: the ρ\rhoρs add up. Specifically:

Theorem 2 (zCDP Sequential Composition)

  • If F1F_1F1​ satisfies ρ1\rho_1ρ1​-zCDP

  • And F2F_2F2​ satisfies ρ2\rho_2ρ2​-zCDP

  • Then their composition satisfies ρ1+ρ2\rho_1+\rho_2ρ1​+ρ2​-zCDP

Finally, zCDP also provides a post-processing property.

Composition under Variants of Differential Privacy¶

Which variant should we use, and when?

The recently-developed variants will yield significantly tighter bounds on privacy cost when:

  • The Gaussian mechanism is used (especially on high-dimensional vectors)

  • The algorithm in question applies the mechanism many times (e.g. hundreds or thousands of times)

To use RDP and zCDP, we typically implement an algorithm in terms of the variant we want to use, and then convert the total privacy cost of running the algorithm back to (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)(ϵ,δ)-differential privacy so that we can compare it to other algorithms.

To see the effect of this strategy, let’s imagine an algorithm that applies the Gaussian mechanism kkk times. We’ll fix values for σ\sigmaσ (i.e. the amount of noise added with the Gaussian mechanism in each of the kkk iterations) and δ\deltaδ, and then compare the final ϵ\epsilonϵs achieved for each variant.

We’ll see that composition under RDP and zCDP result in smaller values of ϵ\epsilonϵ for the same amount of noise added. The algorithm is identical under all variants (i.e. it adds the same amount of noise in each case) - so this means that RDP and zCDP are providing significantly tighter bounds on privacy cost for the same algorithm.

The first thing to note is that using sequential composition under either zCDP or RDP is much better than using advanced composition with (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)(ϵ,δ)-differential privacy. When building iterative algorithms with the Gaussian mechanism, these variants should always be used.

The second thing to note is the difference between zCDP (in orange) and RDP (in green). The ϵ\epsilonϵ for RDP grows linearly in kkk, because we have fixed a value for α\alphaα. The ϵ\epsilonϵ for zCDP is sublinear in kkk, since zCDP effectively considers many α\alphaαs. The two lines touch at some value of kkk, depending on the α\alphaα chosen for RDP (for α=20\alpha = 20α=20, they touch at roughly k=300k=300k=300).

The practical effect of this difference is that α\alphaα must be chosen carefully when using RDP in order to bound privacy cost as tightly as possible. This is usually easy to do, since algorithms are usually parameterized by α\alphaα; as a result, we can simply test multiple values of α\alphaα to see which one results in the smallest corresponding ϵ\epsilonϵ. Since this test is independent of the data (it depends mainly on the privacy parameters we pick, and the number of iterations we want to run), we can test as many values of α\alphaα as we want without paying additional privacy cost. We only need to test a small range of values for α\alphaα - typically in the range between 2 and 100 - to find a minimum. This is the approach taken in most practical implementations, including Google’s differentially private version of Tensorflow.
